# Unit 01 — Economics of Business

Status: curriculum + production authoring blueprint  
Parent: `docs/CURRICULUM_V1.md`  
Program: Business Core

## 1. Unit thesis

Managers rarely need to draw textbook curves for their own sake. They need to understand **what changes behavior at the margin**: what customers do when price changes, what suppliers do when incentives change, which costs matter for the next decision, how market structure changes bargaining power and how competitors may respond.

This unit turns foundational microeconomics into a compact managerial toolkit.

**Unit promise:**

> After this unit, a learner should be able to diagnose a market, identify the relevant economic mechanism and make a pricing or competitive decision without hiding behind averages or intuition alone.

## 2. Research synthesis

The unit is an original Skala synthesis of publicly described core themes from top business schools:

- Wharton Microeconomics for Managers covers supply, demand, consumer behavior, production/cost, competitive equilibrium, monopoly pricing, price discrimination and bundling; its advanced variant adds sophisticated pricing and strategic interaction with competitors.
- MIT Sloan Economic Analysis for Business Decisions explicitly centers supply/demand and game theory, applying them to value propositions, pricing, competition and cooperation.
- INSEAD places `Prices & Markets` in the first period of its MBA core.
- Chicago Booth requires a microeconomics foundation before broader functional and strategy work.
- Stanford GSB includes managerial economics within its broader analytical/management foundations and calibrates some foundational coursework to student background.

Skala should preserve the rigor but teach through business decisions rather than imitate semester-course sequencing.

## 3. Unit structure

Recommended total active time: **80–100 minutes**, normally consumed over multiple days.

| # | Skill | Target time | Primary mode |
|---|---|---:|---|
| 01 | Supply & Demand | 7 min | interactive market model |
| 02 | Opportunity Cost | 6 min | forced-choice decision |
| 03 | Marginal Thinking | 7 min | incremental decision table |
| 04 | Marginal Cost | 7 min | cost simulator |
| 05 | Elasticity | 8 min | price-response simulator |
| 06 | Incentives | 7 min | mechanism / behavior simulation |
| 07 | Market Structure | 8 min | diagnostic classification |
| 08 | Pricing & Price Discrimination | 9 min | segmentation/pricing lab |
| 09 | Competitive Interaction & Game Theory | 9 min | payoff / response simulation |
| 10 | Checkpoint — Market Decision | 15–20 min | integrated mini-case |

No skill should become a slideshow. Each must create at least one evidence-producing learner action.

## 4. Unit mastery targets

A learner completing the unit should be able to:

1. Separate a movement along a demand/supply curve from a structural shift in the market.
2. Identify the best forgone alternative in a decision rather than treating sunk or accounting cost as opportunity cost.
3. Frame a decision around incremental benefits and costs.
4. Distinguish average cost from marginal cost and recognize capacity-step effects.
5. Interpret elasticity directionally and quantitatively enough to reason about price/revenue trade-offs.
6. Predict how incentives can create intended and unintended behavior.
7. Diagnose the economic consequences of competition, differentiation, entry barriers and market power.
8. Design a basic segmented pricing logic without confusing segmentation with arbitrary discrimination.
9. Anticipate competitor response instead of evaluating a move in isolation.
10. Integrate these tools into one coherent market recommendation.

# Skill 01 — Supply & Demand

Graph anchor: `supply-demand`  
Curriculum mapping: EXISTS → needs production-quality expansion

## Capability

Given a market change, identify whether demand or supply moved, predict the direction of equilibrium price/quantity and explain the mechanism.

## Core concepts

- demand as willingness and ability to buy at different prices;
- supply as willingness and ability to sell at different prices;
- equilibrium as a coordination outcome, not a moral optimum;
- movement along a curve vs shift of the curve;
- substitutes and complements;
- input-cost/productivity shocks;
- shortages and surpluses as signals of disequilibrium.

## Common misconception

> “Demand increased because more units were sold after the price fell.”

The learner must distinguish **quantity demanded changing because price changed** from **demand itself shifting because something else changed**.

## Learning experience

### A. Cold open — 30 sec

A fictional subscription service cuts price 20% and sign-ups increase. Ask:

**Did demand increase?**

Options: yes / no / insufficient information.

Commit before reveal.

### B. Interactive market — 2 min

A simple supply-demand diagram where the learner applies shocks:

- competitor exits;
- consumer income rises;
- cloud/input cost falls;
- substitute becomes cheaper;
- regulation limits capacity.

The learner predicts `price ↑/↓/ambiguous` and `quantity ↑/↓/ambiguous` before the visualization moves.

### C. Business interpretation — 2 min

Scenario: a premium gym sees memberships rise after lowering price, while a nearby competitor closes in the same week.

Task: separate the **price effect** from the **demand shift** and state what additional evidence would identify each mechanism.

### D. Transfer check — 1 min

New market, different industry. Classify a shock and predict direction.

## Evidence

- `diagnostic`: distinction between demand and quantity demanded;
- `application`: market-shock reasoning;
- `recall`: directional transfer.

# Skill 02 — Opportunity Cost

Graph anchor: `opportunity-cost`  
Curriculum mapping: EXISTS → expand interaction quality

## Capability

Identify the economically relevant alternative being sacrificed by a choice.

## Core concepts

- opportunity cost = value of the best forgone alternative;
- accounting cost vs economic cost;
- sunk cost is not automatically relevant to the next decision;
- scarce resources include time, attention, capacity and capital;
- the relevant alternative changes with constraints.

## Common misconception

> “If an asset is already paid for, using it is free.”

## Learning experience

### A. Forced choice

A company owns an unused office floor. Three options:

- use it for a new internal team;
- rent it externally;
- keep it empty for expected expansion.

The interface initially hides monetary labels and asks what information determines the opportunity cost.

### B. Reveal

Show market rent, expected expansion probability and relocation costs. Learner identifies the highest-value forgone alternative.

### C. Sunk-cost trap

The company already spent on renovations. Ask whether that changes the next decision and why.

### D. Transfer

Apply opportunity cost to an executive choosing between two product initiatives competing for the same engineering capacity.

## Evidence

- `application`: identifies best forgone alternative;
- `reasoning`: rejects irrelevant sunk-cost framing;
- `transfer`: recognizes non-cash opportunity cost.

# Skill 03 — Marginal Thinking

Graph anchor: NEW

## Capability

Evaluate the next unit, customer, employee, campaign or investment increment rather than relying on totals or averages.

## Core concepts

- marginal benefit;
- marginal cost;
- incremental revenue and incremental cost;
- decision rule: continue while expected marginal benefit exceeds expected marginal cost, subject to risk/constraints;
- averages can hide deterioration at the margin;
- past fixed cost should not dominate an incremental decision.

## Common misconception

> “The business is profitable overall, so every additional customer is good growth.”

## Learning experience

### A. Incremental table

A marketplace is considering expanding paid acquisition. The learner sees five acquisition bands:

| Band | New customers | Incremental contribution | Incremental acquisition cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 1,000 | 80 | 45 |
| B | 1,000 | 75 | 55 |
| C | 1,000 | 68 | 66 |
| D | 1,000 | 60 | 72 |
| E | 1,000 | 52 | 85 |

Values are illustrative per customer. Ask where expansion should stop and what information could change that decision.

### B. Average-vs-margin reveal

Show that average campaign ROI can remain positive even after the newest acquisition band destroys value.

### C. Counterfactual

Retention improves for Band D. Learner updates the decision.

## Evidence

- `application`: chooses economically defensible stopping point;
- `adaptability`: changes decision after marginal economics change.

# Skill 04 — Marginal Cost

Graph anchor: `marginal-cost`  
Curriculum mapping: EXISTS → deepen

## Capability

Estimate what actually changes in cost when one more unit of activity is added, including nonlinear capacity steps.

## Core concepts

- marginal vs average vs fixed cost;
- variable cost drivers;
- spare capacity;
- step-fixed costs and threshold effects;
- economies/diseconomies at the relevant range;
- why software can have low distribution cost but non-zero service/inference/support cost.

## Common misconception

> “Fixed costs never matter at the margin.”

They may not change for the next unit, but can become incremental when capacity thresholds are crossed.

## Learning experience

### A. Cost simulator

Slider: `customers 1,000 → 20,000`.

Live outputs:

- infrastructure;
- payment/transaction cost;
- support labor;
- fixed platform team;
- total cost;
- average cost;
- marginal cost.

At 12,000 customers a new support team becomes necessary, producing a visible step.

### B. Prediction before threshold

Before the slider crosses 12,000, ask learner what happens to marginal and average cost.

### C. Transfer

Compare a digital service, restaurant and consulting team. Identify the operative marginal-cost driver in each.

## Evidence

- `application`: cost-driver identification;
- `quantitative`: correct marginal interpretation;
- `transfer`: recognizes capacity effects.

# Skill 05 — Elasticity

Graph anchor: `elasticity`  
Curriculum mapping: EXISTS → deepen quantitative interaction

## Capability

Reason about how demand responds to price and use that response to evaluate revenue/pricing decisions.

## Core concepts

- price elasticity of demand;
- percentage change in quantity relative to percentage change in price;
- elastic vs inelastic demand;
- elasticity varies by segment, horizon, alternatives and context;
- revenue implication of price changes;
- observed response is not automatically causal elasticity.

Core expression:

`elasticity ≈ % change in quantity / % change in price`

Use absolute magnitude for simple managerial classification while retaining the negative demand relationship conceptually.

## Common misconception

> “We increased price 10% and volume fell 4%, therefore elasticity is always 0.4.”

The observed short-run response can be contaminated by seasonality, selection, anticipation or concurrent changes.

## Learning experience

### A. Price-response simulator

Learner changes price and sees demand curves for three segments:

- business users;
- occasional users;
- highly substitutable users.

Revenue updates live.

### B. Predict first

Before each change, learner predicts which segment loses the most volume.

### C. Evidence challenge

A real-looking A/B summary is shown. Learner identifies whether it supports a causal price-response claim.

### D. Transfer

Choose which of three products likely has more elastic demand and state why.

## Evidence

- `quantitative`: elasticity classification;
- `application`: price/revenue decision;
- `evidence_use`: recognizes causal limitations.

# Skill 06 — Incentives

Graph anchor: `incentives`  
Curriculum mapping: EXISTS → deepen behavioral mechanism

## Capability

Predict how people will respond to a reward, penalty, metric or constraint — including unintended responses.

## Core concepts

- people respond to changes in marginal rewards/costs;
- principal-agent tension;
- measurable proxy vs true objective;
- gaming and substitution;
- incentives interact with information and constraints;
- financial incentives are only one category.

## Common misconception

> “If we reward the desired metric, the desired outcome will improve.”

## Learning experience

### A. Mechanism lab

A customer-support organization pays bonuses for `tickets closed per hour`.

Learner predicts changes in:

- closure speed;
- repeat contacts;
- customer satisfaction;
- difficult-ticket avoidance.

### B. Simulation

Adjust weights between speed, first-contact resolution and customer outcome. The simulated team behavior changes.

### C. Counterfactual

Agents receive better information but the same metric. Ask what changes and what does not.

### D. Transfer

Diagnose an incentive problem in sales or product metrics.

## Evidence

- `application`: predicts intended/unintended behavior;
- `design`: proposes better incentive architecture;
- `adaptability`: revises after information changes.

# Skill 07 — Market Structure

Graph anchor: `market-structure`  
Curriculum mapping: EXISTS → production expansion

## Capability

Diagnose how concentration, differentiation, entry barriers and bargaining power shape pricing freedom and expected profitability.

## Core concepts

- competitive markets;
- differentiated competition;
- concentrated/oligopolistic markets;
- monopoly/market power as an economic condition, not simply being “big”;
- entry barriers;
- substitutes;
- buyer and supplier power;
- market boundaries matter.

## Common misconception

> “Few competitors automatically means attractive economics.”

## Learning experience

### A. Classification

Show four simplified markets with different:

- number of competitors;
- switching costs;
- differentiation;
- entry costs;
- buyer concentration.

Learner ranks expected pricing power.

### B. Market-definition challenge

A company appears dominant in a narrow category but small against broader substitutes. Learner chooses the defensible market boundary.

### C. Transfer

Predict what happens to industry margins after a major entry barrier falls.

## Evidence

- `diagnostic`: structure classification;
- `application`: market-definition reasoning;
- `transfer`: consequence of structural change.

# Skill 08 — Pricing & Price Discrimination

Graph anchors: `pricing-strategy`, `elasticity`  
Curriculum mapping: EXPAND

## Capability

Choose when and how differentiated prices can capture value across customers without destroying adoption, fairness or strategic coherence.

## Core concepts

- willingness to pay;
- market power and pricing discretion;
- segment differences;
- first/second/third-degree price discrimination as conceptual families, taught without requiring labels as the main learning objective;
- versioning, quantity pricing and fences;
- arbitrage/resale constraints;
- fairness, trust and regulation as practical boundaries;
- pricing metric vs price level.

## Common misconception

> “Price discrimination just means charging every customer as much as possible.”

## Learning experience

### A. Pricing lab

A B2B product has three customer segments with different value, usage and alternatives. Learner can set:

- one flat price;
- tiered plans;
- usage pricing;
- segment-specific offers with eligibility rules.

The simulation shows adoption, revenue, contribution and perceived fairness risk.

### B. Constraint reveal

Introduce resale/arbitrage or regulation. Learner redesigns the pricing architecture.

### C. Transfer

Identify why airline advance-purchase pricing and volume enterprise pricing separate willingness to pay using different mechanisms.

## Evidence

- `application`: coherent pricing architecture;
- `tradeoff`: capture vs adoption/fairness;
- `adaptability`: redesign after constraint.

# Skill 09 — Competitive Interaction & Game Theory

Graph anchor: NEW

## Capability

Anticipate how another rational actor may respond to a strategic move and identify when individual incentives produce a poor joint outcome.

## Core concepts

- actions are interdependent;
- best response;
- dominant strategy intuition;
- Nash equilibrium intuition;
- sequential moves and commitment;
- repeated interaction/reputation;
- cooperation vs unilateral incentive;
- credible threats and responses.

The unit should teach the intuition before terminology.

## Common misconception

> “If a move is profitable assuming the competitor does nothing, it is a good move.”

## Learning experience

### A. Price-war simulation

Two competitors choose `hold price` or `cut price`.

Learner commits to an action, then sees the rival's incentive and resulting payoff matrix.

### B. Repeated round

Run three rounds. The learner sees how future interaction can change incentives without implying illegal coordination.

### C. Sequential move

One player can commit capacity before the other enters. Ask whether the commitment is credible and economically rational.

### D. Transfer

Apply best-response reasoning to a non-price move such as distribution exclusivity or product bundling.

## Evidence

- `application`: predicts best response;
- `strategic_reasoning`: incorporates reaction into original choice;
- `transfer`: recognizes interdependence outside price competition.

# Checkpoint 01 — Market Decision

Working title: **Survia — Price the Route**

This is an original synthetic mini-case.

## Premise

Survia operates intercity transport routes between several regional cities. One flagship route has strong weekend demand, weak midweek utilization and two customer segments with different flexibility. A lower-cost competitor is entering the route next quarter. Fuel/input costs have risen, but Survia still has spare capacity on many departures.

Management is considering:

1. a uniform 12% price increase;
2. lower midweek prices and higher peak prices;
3. matching the entrant's low headline fare;
4. keeping prices and reducing frequency;
5. a segmented fare architecture with restrictions.

## Evidence released in stages

### Stage 1 — Market

- historical price/volume by departure;
- segment mix;
- route capacity/utilization;
- competitor announcement.

Learner must identify the first economic questions before seeing all data.

### Stage 2 — Economics

- incremental fuel/servicing cost per passenger;
- step cost when an additional vehicle/departure is required;
- estimated price response by segment;
- alternative transportation options.

Learner submits an initial recommendation.

### Stage 3 — Competitor response

The entrant announces it will match selected promotional fares but cannot add much peak capacity.

Learner revises or defends the recommendation.

### Stage 4 — Incentive wrinkle

Sales managers receive bonuses based on passenger volume, not contribution. Discounting has increased in one channel.

Learner identifies whether the incentive system affects the observed evidence and the recommended policy.

## Required outputs

1. Diagnose the market mechanism.
2. Identify which costs are relevant at the margin.
3. Interpret elasticity differences by segment.
4. Recommend a pricing architecture.
5. Anticipate competitor response.
6. Explain the role of the sales incentive.
7. State the key uncertainty that would most change the decision.

## Checkpoint rubric

| Dimension | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| Economic framing | separates demand shifts, capacity and price response correctly |
| Marginal reasoning | uses incremental rather than average economics |
| Elasticity | distinguishes segment response and revenue implications |
| Incentives | identifies metric-driven behavior or evidence distortion |
| Market structure | incorporates entrant capacity/substitutes rather than competitor count alone |
| Strategic interaction | anticipates plausible rival response |
| Decision coherence | recommendation follows from the evidence and stated assumptions |
| Adaptability | updates when Stage 3/4 information changes the economics |

The checkpoint should create `application` evidence for multiple underlying skills but should not mark all of them demonstrated after one attempt.

  - `market-structure → competitive-interaction`;
  - `competitive-interaction → strategy/industry-structure`.

## Recommended sequencing rule

Skills 01–06 form the minimum economic foundation. Skills 07–09 can adapt based on diagnostic mastery, but the checkpoint should not unlock until the learner has sufficient evidence in:

- marginal thinking;
- elasticity;
- market structure;
- incentives.

# 6. Editorial and interaction standard

For this unit:

- explanations should prefer plain managerial language before technical labels;
- formulas appear only where they improve decisions;
- every numerical interaction must explain units and assumptions;
- examples should span digital and physical businesses;
- at least three skills must use manipulable visuals rather than text/forms only;
- do not reuse the exact `think → reveal → application → challenge → transfer` presentation for every skill;
- ES and EN versions must be authored explicitly, not machine-translated at runtime;
- quantitative questions need deterministic objective scoring where possible;
- open-ended evaluation should score reasoning dimensions, not writing style.
